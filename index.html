How we estimate indoor temperature

NightMaa estimates tonight's indoor temperature from the live outdoor forecast (Open-Meteo) plus simple adjustments for the home: a tin or asbestos roof adds about +2.5°C, a concrete slab +1.5°C, tile or thatch +0.5°C; upper floors and poor ventilation add a little more; a cooler or AC subtracts. These adjustments are informed by published Indian housing-heat research, including sensor studies showing that homes stay several degrees hotter than the street at night. This is a transparent estimate, not a sensor measurement — the real temperature in a given home will differ. The risk bands are a guide for action, not a medical diagnosis.

For ASHA workers

A one-minute check, built for the home visit you already make. Open this page on any phone, tap the mother's village or town, answer four questions about her home — roof, floor, ventilation, cooling — and you will see tonight's indoor heat risk for that home, in Hindi or English, with a simple plan you can explain in a minute. Why this matters — the research

NightMaa is built on published evidence, not guesswork.

  • The heat inside homes at night is extreme — and unmeasured. Sensors placed in 50 low- and middle-income homes in Chennai (Climate Trends study, Oct 2025–Apr 2026) found indoor temperatures frequently above 32°C and rarely below 31°C even at night, peaking around 8–9 pm at ~34.7°C as walls give back the day's heat. The worst homes spent 5,700–5,800 hours above 32°C — and the study did not even cover peak summer. Ground Report — “Inside India's homes, heat never leaves — not even at night”
  • Heat in pregnancy carries real risk. A Nature Medicine systematic review and meta-analysis of 198 studies across 66 countries found the odds of preterm birth rise by about 4% for every +1°C (OR 1.04), and by 26% during heatwaves (OR 1.26). Nature Medicine — heat exposure and maternal, foetal and neonatal health outcomes
  • Indian evidence, from Tamil Nadu. In a prospective study of 800 pregnant women doing moderate-to-heavy physical work, those with high heat exposure had 2.4× the adjusted odds of miscarriage and 2.3× the odds of any adverse pregnancy outcome (BJOG, 2024). PubMed — occupational heat exposure and pregnancy outcomes, Tamil Nadu cohort
  • The policy blind spot. India has more than 300 city Heat Action Plans — but none of them mandates monitoring indoor temperature, and indoor nighttime heat is not systematically monitored in any major Indian city. Every official warning still describes the outdoor afternoon, not the bedroom at midnight. Climate Trends study coverage — the indoor-monitoring gap

Important: NightMaa provides heat-risk information to support decisions by families and frontline health workers. It is not a medical diagnosis, and it does not replace advice from a doctor, nurse or ASHA worker. Indoor temperatures are estimates from outdoor weather and housing type — not sensor readings.
